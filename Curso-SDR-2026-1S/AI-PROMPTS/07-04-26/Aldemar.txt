A. Executive summary

For this project, the requirements and feasibility phase should end with a very disciplined conclusion:

The laboratory is feasible with a low-cost RTL-SDR, an FM whip antenna, and a personal computer.
It is not feasible to promise professional-grade absolute accuracy in power, OBW, or carrier-frequency estimation without calibration, controlled gain policy, and explicit uncertainty bounds.
The design must therefore be positioned as an educational receive-only FM sensing lab, not as a regulatory monitoring instrument and not as a transmitter under any circumstance. In Colombia, the FM broadcast band is allocated to radiodifusión sonora FM in 88–108 MHz, and spectrum use is subject to state control and permission frameworks; your project should stay strictly in passive reception and educational analysis.

A realistic scope is:

Scan and visualize the 88–108 MHz FM band.
Detect channel presence/activity.
Estimate center frequency, occupied bandwidth (OBW), and relative/approximate received power per detected station.
Extract educational FM indicators such as stereo pilot visibility, noise floor behavior, PSD shape, adjacent-channel effects, and gain/saturation effects.
Teach students the practical gap between theory and hardware reality: 8-bit ADC limits, tuner behavior, sample-rate tradeoffs, DC spike, IQ imbalance, aliasing, gain staging, and poor-signal-area effects. RTL-SDR-class devices are receive-only and typically operate stably around 2.56 MS/s, with 8-bit digitization and limited dynamic range relative to lab-grade instruments.

The key systems-engineering decision in feasibility is this:

Do not define the lab around “accurate FM station metrology everywhere.”
Define it around repeatable educational sensing with quantified limitations.

That choice prevents scope inflation, budget overruns, and user dissatisfaction.

B. Requirements specification and warnings
1. System mission

Mission statement

Design a receive-only educational SDR laboratory for sensing the 88–108 MHz FM broadcast band using RTL-SDR + whip antenna + COTS PC, in a poor reception area, producing repeatable measurements and educational workflows for PSD, occupancy, channel activity, FM characteristics, and approximate signal metrics.

2. Stakeholders

Primary users:

students learning SDR, RF, and DSP
instructors preparing labs and experiments
project maintainers extending the platform

Secondary stakeholders:

institution/lab manager controlling cost and reproducibility
legal/compliance reviewers ensuring receive-only operation

5. Measurement requirements

You explicitly want OBW, center frequency, and power for each FM station. These must be written carefully.

5.1 Center frequency

Requirement:

Estimate center frequency from PSD with a defined method such as:
peak frequency after smoothing, or
power centroid within detected station mask, or
hybrid peak + centroid method

Warning:

In poor reception, multipath, low SNR, adjacent-channel overlap, and RTL tuner/frequency error can bias the estimate.
5.2 Occupied bandwidth (OBW)

Requirement:

Define OBW as the bandwidth containing 99% of integrated power within the selected station span. That is the standard engineering definition used in many test contexts.

Warning:

On an RTL-SDR, OBW from PSD is highly sensitive to:
FFT resolution
windowing
smoothing
station-mask boundaries
nearby channels
gain saturation
poor SNR

So the requirement should be:

Educational OBW estimate with documented uncertainty, not a compliance-grade OBW claim.
5.3 Power

Requirement:

Report:
relative power in dBFS or normalized dB
optional approximate received power in dBm only after calibration

Warning:

Without a calibration chain, direct dBm claims are weak.
RTL-SDR gain settings are coarse and front-end behavior is nonlinear under strong-signal conditions.
A poor reception area reduces overload risk, but it increases uncertainty and false negatives.
6. Explicit warnings that must appear in the requirements baseline

These warnings should be part of the specification, not footnotes:

Warning 1: Receive-only constraint

Any design assumption involving RF transmission, replay, active probing, or rebroadcast is out of scope and should be rejected immediately.

Warning 2: RTL-SDR measurement realism

RTL-SDR-class hardware uses 8-bit ADC conversion and has practical sample-rate limits; stable operation is commonly around 2.56 MS/s, while higher rates may drop samples. That means limited dynamic range and vulnerability to overload, quantization effects, and measurement bias.

Warning 3: Full-band capture is not instantaneous

The FM band is 20 MHz wide, but the RTL-SDR instantaneous bandwidth is much smaller. So the band must be covered by:

segmented scanning, or
overlapping stepped acquisitions

That introduces dwell-time and temporal-consistency tradeoffs.

Warning 4: Poor reception area changes the design

A poor reception area is not just a performance issue; it changes requirements:

detection thresholds must be conservative
false negatives become more likely
antenna placement matters more
long dwell and averaging become important
“power per station” becomes less stable across time
Warning 5: DC spike, IQ imbalance, and aliasing are mandatory design concerns

Any architecture that ignores these is not acceptable. They directly affect center bins, symmetry, station detection, and power estimation.

C. Analysis for the technical, financial, operational, legal, and scheduling constraints to prevent cost overruns, risks, and user dissatisfaction
C1. Technical feasibility
1. Is the system technically feasible?

Yes, with an important caveat:

It is feasible as an educational spectrum-sensing system
It is not feasible as a precision regulatory measurement platform using only RTL-SDR + whip + PC

That distinction is essential.

2. Recommended sensing architecture

Because the FM band is 88–108 MHz and the RTL-SDR cannot capture all 20 MHz at once, the technical baseline should be:

Architecture option A — stepped wideband scan

Divide 88–108 MHz into overlapping spans
Example: center frequencies every 1.6 to 2.0 MHz with overlap
At each step:
acquire IQ
apply DC mitigation
FFT/PSD
discard unreliable edge bins
stitch spans into a band panorama

Architecture option B — channelized scan

Predefine channel centers or subbands
Measure occupancy and station metrics per channel
Better for stable classroom exercises, less good for exploratory spectrum view

For education, I recommend:

Panorama scan for visualization
Channel-focused re-acquisition for per-station OBW/frequency/power

That is more technically sound than trying to extract all station metrics from one coarse panorama.

3. Required signal-processing chain

A concrete chain should be part of the requirements phase:

Acquisition
fixed sample rate, e.g. 2.048 or 2.4 MS/s for conservative operation, or 2.56 MS/s if stable on the chosen PC and USB path
manual gain mode
fixed dwell per step
Preprocessing
remove DC offset in I/Q
optional notch or bin suppression at DC if center-tuned artifact remains
optional IQ imbalance mitigation if image asymmetry is severe
Windowing + FFT
Hann or Blackman-Harris window
define FFT sizes such as 4096, 8192, or 16384 depending on dwell and CPU budget
PSD estimation
Welch averaging preferred for stability
record RBW-equivalent bin width from Fs / NFFT
Artifact control
reject edge bins near tuner roll-off
flag scans with clipping or overload symptoms
detect abnormal symmetry or DC contamination
Detection
adaptive noise floor estimation
station mask extraction
peak grouping / connected-component logic on thresholded PSD
Per-station metrics
center frequency
OBW
channel power / relative power
occupancy decision
optional stereo-pilot evidence near 19 kHz in demodulated audio for teaching
Validation/metadata
store settings and confidence flags

This is concrete enough to be implementable and still low-cost.

4. Mandatory RTL-SDR limitations and how they affect requirements
4.1 8-bit ADC behavior

Effect:

limited dynamic range
quantization noise
overload sensitivity
weak discrimination between strong and weak stations in the same scene

Requirement implication:

define acceptable operation only in non-clipped conditions
include overload detection in software
do not promise absolute power accuracy without calibration
4.2 Sample-rate constraints

Effect:

stable high-rate streaming is limited
dropped samples corrupt PSD consistency

Requirement implication:

choose a validated sample rate and freeze it in baseline testing
prioritize stability over maximum bandwidth
4.3 Tuning limitations and LO-related artifacts

Effect:

center-frequency tuning may place strong stations near the DC artifact
tuning step strategy matters
edge-of-span response is not equally trustworthy

Requirement implication:

use overlapping spans
crop center bins or shift tuning so stations are not always centered on DC
crop edge bins before stitching
4.4 Gain control limitations

Effect:

auto gain makes measurements non-reproducible
manual gain too high causes clipping; too low loses weak stations

Requirement implication:

use a fixed manual gain policy
define one or a few approved gain presets for lab experiments
4.5 DC spike

Effect:

corrupts center bins
biases station detection and power
can look like a false narrowband emission

Requirement implication:

DC mitigation is mandatory
never trust center bins blindly
tune so suspected stations are not exactly on DC whenever possible
4.6 IQ imbalance

Effect:

mirrored artifacts
asymmetrical spectra
possible false station interpretations

Requirement implication:

include a simple IQ-balance diagnostic
document that image artifacts are hardware-induced, not necessarily real stations
4.7 Aliasing and out-of-band contamination

Effect:

misleading spectral energy
especially risky with cheap front ends and strong nearby signals

Requirement implication:

edge-bin rejection
optional FM broadcast bandpass filter only if justified by observed interference
do not add expensive front-end hardware unless the baseline fails
5. Poor reception area: feasibility impact

This constraint is central.

In a poor reception area, the system is still educationally useful, but the success criteria must shift from “see all stations strongly” to:

detect available stations above threshold
observe how antenna position changes PSD
study dwell-time and averaging tradeoffs
compare weak-signal detection performance under different gains and windows
analyze false negatives and time variability

This actually strengthens the educational value, because students will learn:

propagation
noise floor variability
antenna placement sensitivity
detection threshold design
measurement uncertainty
6. Suggested technical acceptance targets

These are realistic targets for the requirements phase:

Metric	Realistic target
Band coverage	88–108 MHz via stepped scan
Stable runtime	30–60 min without crashes or repeated sample drops
Scan repeatability	Similar panorama shape across repeated scans under same setup
Center-frequency estimate	within a few kHz to tens of kHz for strong stations, depending on calibration and SNR
OBW repeatability	within a defined tolerance band for strong isolated stations
Power repeatability	better for relative comparisons than absolute dBm
Educational usability	students can explain at least 5 hardware artifacts from observed data

Be careful: exact numeric accuracy for frequency and power depends heavily on calibration and local signal conditions, so any tighter target should be validated experimentally first.

C4. Legal feasibility

The legal framing should be conservative.

1. Receive-only legal posture

Your stated use is correct:

passive sensing
educational/laboratory use
no transmission

That must be enforced in both documentation and implementation.

In Colombia, the spectrum is a public resource under state management, and the FM band is allocated to broadcasting services. Your project should therefore remain strictly on the side of passive reception and analysis, not operation of transmitting equipment or imitation of official monitoring authority.

2. Legal requirements for the feasibility phase

The requirements set should include:

no RF transmission capability in the design
no recommendation to radiate test signals on-air
no claims of regulatory enforcement-grade measurement
no publication of results as official spectrum compliance findings
respect for local rules on use of recorded content, privacy, and institutional policies if audio is demodulated or stored
3. Important legal caution

Pure FM broadcast reception is generally lower risk than transmitting, but legal issues may still arise around:

redistribution of demodulated content
institutional use of recordings
presenting measurements as official evidence
adding active emitters for testing without authorization

So the legal baseline should say:

The laboratory is for passive signal observation, academic analysis, and internal educational use. It is not an authorized spectrum-enforcement platform and must not radiate RF signals.

C8. Validation plan

A design that skips validation should be rejected.

1. Validation philosophy

Validation here does not mean “certify like a regulator.”
It means:

prove the lab is repeatable
quantify error sources
show when outputs are trustworthy
show when they are not
2. Required validation tests
Test V1 — Device stability
run repeated acquisitions at selected sample rates
measure dropped samples, crashes, or corrupted scans
accept only stable configurations
Test V2 — Repeatability
same setup, same antenna position, multiple scans
compare PSD panorama and extracted station list
Test V3 — Gain sensitivity
repeat scans at several manual gain settings
observe station count, power ranking, and saturation behavior
Test V4 — Antenna placement sensitivity
indoor positions near window / away from window / elevated position
quantify how poor reception area affects performance
Test V5 — DC spike robustness
place strong station near center-tuned bin
verify the algorithm does not report false OBW/power
Test V6 — Frequency estimate sanity
compare detected station frequencies against known broadcast allocations or stable observed carrier locations in the local band
document offsets and repeatability
Test V7 — OBW consistency
for strong isolated stations, repeat OBW estimate over time
report spread of estimated bandwidth
Test V8 — Power reporting validity
confirm that “relative power” is stable enough for comparison
only enable dBm reporting after calibration evidence
3. Validation metrics to require

Minimum metrics:

scan completion success rate
dropped-sample incidence
station detection repeatability
frequency-estimation repeatability
OBW repeatability
relative-power repeatability
false-detection count
number of scans flagged as unreliable
4. Confidence labeling

Each station measurement should carry a confidence class such as:

High confidence: isolated strong station, no clipping, low DC contamination
Medium confidence: moderate SNR or minor adjacency
Low confidence: near DC, near edge, weak, or overlap-contaminated

That is realistic and educational.
